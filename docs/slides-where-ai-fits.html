Block 1 · How LLMs work

What we mean by AI

Artificial intelligence

Any computer system doing a task we would otherwise call intelligent. Spell check, fraud detection, chess.

Machine learning

Systems that learn the pattern from examples instead of being given the rules.

Deep learning

Machine learning with very large networks, trained on very large piles of data.

Large language models

Deep learning trained on an enormous amount of text to do one thing: predict the next word. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot.

When people say "AI" today they almost always mean the inner box. Everything in the next fifteen minutes follows from that one job: predict the next word.

Thirty seconds on the boxes, outside in. AI is the big umbrella, and most of it is old and boring: spell check, spam filters, the fraud alert on your card. Machine learning is the part that learns from examples. Deep learning is machine learning at very large scale. Large language models are deep learning trained on text, and they are what everyone means by AI right now. If anyone asks how the people who regulate and fund this define it: the National AI Initiative Act (15 U.S.C. 9401) calls it a machine-based system that makes predictions, recommendations or decisions for human-defined objectives. NIH's data science plan calls it the power of a machine to copy intelligent human behavior. University guidelines, Utah's for example, say a system that performs tasks that typically require human intelligence. Notice that all three define it by what it does. None says where its knowledge comes from. Hold that; it comes back in block 3. Then: the one job is predict the next word. Let's watch it do that.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

Build a sentence one token at a time

Click the next word. Each choice shifts what comes next, exactly like an LLM.

No right answer, only probabilities. This is why the same prompt gives different output each time.

Ask for a volunteer to call out words. Build the sentence twice and get two different endings. Three things to land here. One: the model is picking the next token from a probability distribution over everything in its window. Two: the numbers above the words are attention weights, how much each earlier word matters for the next pick. Three: the bar chart is the whole space of sentences the model could have produced. Your path is one of sixty-four. Click the chart for the gradient descent sidebar if someone asks how it learned the weights. The practical consequence: run the same extraction twice and you can get different answers. That is not a bug you can configure away. It is the mechanism.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

Three ways to pick a number between 1 and 10

Random Every number equally likely. Dice, RANDBETWEEN, a sampler.
Human Skips the obvious ones, drifts toward numbers that feel random. 7 wins.
LLM Trained on humans, then tuned to be confident. The human bump becomes a spike.

The least surprising graph in the world, once you know how the model was trained.

After a chart shared by Adam Zachary Wasserman. Lesson from AI4RA Tip of the Week #25.

Hold this next to the tally you just built. Your bars are the red curve. Walk the three curves. Random is what people think they are asking for. Human is what people actually do: nobody says 1 or 10, few say 5, lots say 7. The model learned from millions of humans doing exactly that, so it inherits the bump. Then it was tuned, over and over, to give the answer people rate highest, and that tuning pushes weight toward the single most likely answer. Gradient descent rewards the peak. The bump becomes a spike. Three mechanisms to say out loud. One: it learned from people, and people are bad at random. Two: training to be confident sharpens the favorites. Three: every new chat starts from scratch, so it cannot notice it has said 7 a hundred times today; inside one chat it may avoid repeating itself and moves to the next most human answer, usually 3. Then the sentence that carries the whole morning: it is not rolling dice. It is predicting what a person would say. If someone asks how it learned: click the bar chart on the token-builder slide for the gradient descent sidebar. Same idea, same picture.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

Where "most likely" bites in research administration

When you asked for random and got "most likely"
  • "Randomly select ten awards for a spot check." The pick looks random and follows hidden patterns. That is a defensibility problem, not a statistics problem.
  • Transactions for an audit sample. Files for a quality review. Participants for a survey.
  • AI-written training quizzes put the correct answer in the same few positions, often B. Human test-writers do the same, and it learned from them.
What to do instead
  • Use a random tool when randomness matters. RANDBETWEEN, a generator, your audit software's sampler.
  • Make the model run code and show it. "Use code to randomly select 10 rows from this spreadsheet. Show me the code so I can verify the selection."
  • Check AI-written quizzes. Count which letter is correct and shuffle if it is lopsided.
  • Write down the method. An audit sample should be reproducible by someone who was not in the room.

The most likely answer is not the same thing as the right answer.

Dwell on the audit sample. A sample that was "randomly selected" by a chatbot follows the habits of every human who ever wrote the words "random sample" on the internet. If an auditor asks how it was drawn, "I asked the AI" is not a method. The fixes are cheap. A real random function is one cell in Excel. Asking the model to run code and show the code is the same move the whole morning comes back to: make the tool show its work, then verify the work instead of the vibe. That is exactly what the Vandalizer block does with document extraction. Say the callout line once more. Then the next slide gives the room the metaphor to carry all of it: the crowd.

Block 1 · A mental model

You are asking a crowd of a thousand non-experts

Picture a thousand well-read, well-meaning people with no particular expertise. You ask a question, and you get back whatever most of them would say, in one confident voice.

  • They have read almost everything ever published.
  • They have read none of your files unless you hand them over.
  • They answer fast, and they always answer.
  • There is no minority report. The dissenters are averaged away.
  • Ask them for a random number and you get the crowd's favorite.

How do you train a crowd? Pay some people to speak louder.

The callout is the training step. Pretraining assembles the crowd from everything ever written. Fine-tuning and reinforcement from human feedback do not teach the crowd new facts; they pay the people whose answers got good ratings to speak louder, and the rest to hush. That is why the human bump on the chart became a spike: the crowd's favorite got amplified. It is also why the voice is so confident. The loud ones were rewarded for sounding sure. This is the metaphor to carry out of the room. Not an oracle, not a search engine, not a colleague. A very large crowd of reasonably informed amateurs, polled instantly, reporting the majority view with total confidence. And it settles the cornerstone. A crowd you can consult in seconds is a tremendous thing to have beside you. It is not a replacement for the one person in the building who has read your sponsor's terms. Augment, not replace, is what the metaphor recommends on its own. Everything so far fits it. The token builder is the crowd voting on the next word. The 7 is the crowd's favorite number. The fabricated subaward, coming up, is what the crowd assumes an award summary usually says. And it tells you what to hand them. The crowd has read the whole internet and none of your award terms. If you want them to answer about your award, the award has to be in the question. That is the context window slide, two slides from now.

Block 1 · A mental model

When to ask the crowd, and when not to

Ask the crowd
  • Consensus facts. What an award notice usually contains.
  • Summarizing. A forty-page solicitation into one page.
  • Drafting from a pattern. A routine email to a PI, a budget justification.
  • Brainstorming. Ten ways to phrase a hard message.
  • Finding things. Where a document talks about cost share.
Do not ask the crowd alone
  • Niche or local knowledge. Your sponsor's unusual term, your office's practice.
  • Anything recent. A rule that changed after the model was trained.
  • A claim nobody has made. The crowd can only repeat what it has read.
  • Judgment calls. Allowability, a finding, what you ought to do.
  • Anything that must match twice. Audit samples. Ask twice, get two answers.

The right column is shrinking: models now run code, check their own work, and look things up. It is not empty, and the habit is the same on both sides. Not without checking.

Left column: things a thousand reasonably informed people are genuinely good at. Read the examples, not the headings. Right column: things a crowd is bad at no matter how big, unless someone hands it the source. Your sponsor's unusual term. A rule that changed last year. Whether a claim nobody has made before is true. What you ought to do. And reproducibility: ask the crowd twice, get two answers. The heading says "alone" on purpose; some of these move left once the model is given the record, which is block 3. Say the caveat honestly. Reasoning models, tool use, and code execution are moving items from the right column leftward every year. Multi-step reasoning in particular is much better than it was. The habit to build is not "never," it is "not without checking." Bert will spend an hour on the ethical-judgment row. Point at "Anything recent" and leave it hanging; it is the whole of block 3. Research administration maps onto this cleanly: the documents go left, the determinations go right. The room will sort real tasks this way in block 5.

  2. The project includes a $60,000 subaward to the U.S. Forest Service for field site access.Not in the source. Summaries usually mention a subaward, so it supplied one.

  4. Consistent with the program's standard terms, the award requires a one-to-one institutional cost share.Contradicts the source, which says no cost sharing. What is typical beat what is written.

Confidence is a writing style, not a signal. Every sentence arrived in the same tone.

Give the room thirty seconds. Then reveal. Three fabrications, and they are two different kinds. The subaward and the staffing are gap-filling: the model knows award summaries usually mention these things, so it supplies plausible ones. The cost share is worse: the source says no cost share, and the model overrode it with what is typical. Priors beat evidence. The tell is that all six sentences read identically. Nothing in the prose marks which three were invented. The only way to catch them was to have the source next to it. Hold on to that: it is the whole argument for provenance in the Vandalizer block.

Block 3 · Memory and the record

A story from Plato, 2,400 years ago

In response to Theuth’s gift of writing, Thamus replied:

This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners’ souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves. The specific which you have discovered is an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence, and you give your disciples not truth, but only the semblance of truth; they will be hearers of many things and will have learned nothing; they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing; they will be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the reality.

Thamus thought writing would weaken people's memory, and that leaning on reminders would weaken their reasoning. For a language model it works the other way: its memory is a mishmash of everything it was trained on, and it reasons better when it is handed an authoritative source of data to work from.

Plato, Phaedrus 274c to 275b, written around 370 BCE. Benjamin Jowett’s translation.

Two minutes, told, not read. Socrates tells it to Phaedrus. Theuth brings his inventions to the king and argues for each; writing is the one he is proudest of. Thamus answers that the inventor is the last person to judge what an invention does to the people who use it, and then gives the verdict on the slide. Land on the distinction: memory versus reminding. He meant reminding as the lesser thing. Then turn it on the machine in one breath: a model's memory is its training, enormous, confident, frozen on a date, stale after it. The fix for a machine is the reminding the king refused. Do not say anything yet about why we know his name; that comes at the very end. Then open the page named for him.

Block 3 · Memory and the record

Two kinds of knowing: recalled and accessed

Recalled

What the crowd remembers from training.

  • Frozen at the training cutoff. The regulation it remembers is the one that was written about most, not the one in force.
  • Blurred. An average over everything ever published on the subject.
  • Fills gaps with the most plausible thing, in the same confident voice.

This is where hallucination lives.

Accessed

What the model reads right now from a source you connected.

  • A document in the window. A regulation fetched by section number. A query against your awards. An API response.
  • Current as of the source, and citable: it can point to where it looked.
  • Wrong only if the source is wrong, or the lookup missed. Both are checkable.

Hallucination is recalled knowledge standing in where accessed knowledge was needed. If the answer matters, make it accessed.

Name what the room just watched on the Thamus page. The crowd metaphor, one level deeper. Ask a thousand well-read people what the de minimis rate is and you get what most of them remember reading. Hand one of them the current regulation and ask again, and you get what it says. Same crowd, different kind of answer. Recalled is not useless. It is what makes the model fluent and fast, and for consensus facts it is fine. It is the wrong tool for anything with a date on it, anything institution-specific, and anything with a number that changes. Accessed is the fix, and the room just watched it on the Thamus page. One more way to hand a model a source, and the one most of this room lives in: a table. That is Carl, next.

Wrong!

The most important model is your data model.

AI models come and go. The way your institution organizes, governs, and shares its data decides whether any of them are useful.

Every model on the previous slide will be replaced within two years. Your award table will not be. The question that decides whether AI helps your office is not which model you license. It is whether the data it needs exists in a form it can be given.

Block 4 · The data underneath

Why research administration needs a common language

Today

Three institutions, three solutions, zero shared work

One office writes its effort certification query against its ERP. The next rebuilds it against a different ERP. A third rebuilds it against a custom eRA system. Each office holds the same things, a sponsor, a PI, an award, a budget, and every system names them differently. Every report starts by reconciling them again.

And a free-text Department column, filled in when somebody remembered, silently drops three million dollars from a total.

With a shared language

One solution, and the next thirty institutions run it unchanged

A data model is a shared agreement about what things exist, how they relate, and what they are called, before any database is involved. Map each of your systems to it once, and a query, a dashboard, or an AI workflow written anywhere runs at your institution too.

Block 4 · The data underneath

Data sovereignty: where does the model run?

Vendor-hosted (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft Copilot)

  • The largest, most capable models, with no hardware to buy or maintain
  • Pay per use, scales with the funding cycle
  • Your document leaves the building, and so does the computing. You do not see where it ran or what it cost in energy.
Two tiers. Public text you could post on a bulletin board: a personal key on a free tier is fine, and that is all it is for. Anything from your institution's systems: the account your institution has vetted, never a personal login. Vendor business tiers carry no-training terms; the institution's agreement adds a named contact and an audit trail. FERPA, HIPAA, export-controlled and PII data may not leave under any agreement.

Self-hosted (Llama, GPT-oss, Qwen, and others, through Ollama or vLLM)

  • Data never leaves your house. FERPA, HIPAA, PII, and export-controlled material stay where policy says they stay.
  • You know exactly where the computing is done, by whom, and on what hardware.
  • You know how much energy you are using. A desktop-class server runs a day of extraction for a few kilowatt-hours; cloud inference is bundled and invisible.
  • Fixed cost, up front. Vandalizer and MindRouter run this way at Idaho.
The trade: you own the security, the hardware, and the updates. Smaller models than the cloud frontier, though the gap closes every year.

Cloud for general work, local for anything restricted

  • Enterprise cloud for drafting, summarizing public documents, and high throughput
  • A local server for award files, personnel data, and anything with a regulation attached
  • Where most institutions land, whether they planned to or not
The hard part is governance: which models are approved for which data, written down, and someone whose job it is to enforce it.

Three questions for any AI tool: where is the computing done, what leaves the building, and what agreement covers it when it does.

Brief, but do not skip it. Sovereignty means you decide where your data goes and who can compute on it. Every AI tool answers that question somewhere in its terms of service, and most people never read the answer. Cloud: the best models, and the least visibility. Say the two tiers plainly. A free personal key is for public text, the kind the bulletin-board rule allows, and the Monday Promptulus practice uses exactly that; a free tier's terms can include training on your inputs, which is why nothing from your systems goes through it. For anything exported from your systems, the account your institution has vetted. Most universities already hold a Microsoft or Google agreement that covers Copilot or Gemini even if nobody in the research office knows; the CIO's office can say in one email. And some data cannot go out under any agreement; know which of your documents those are. Local: the benefits are concrete. You know where the computing happened. The data never left. And you can read the energy use off the wall: a desktop-class server does a day of extraction on a few kilowatt-hours, where cloud inference is bundled into a bill you cannot itemize. The cost is that you run it. Hybrid is where everyone ends up. The work is not the hardware, it is the policy that says which model touches which data. If your institution does not have that policy yet, this is a good week to ask who is writing it. The tools we built are provider-agnostic on purpose. Vandalizer will talk to an enterprise cloud model or a local one on the same workflow, so the sovereignty decision and the tool decision stay separate. Hand-off, and the end of the block: the record can be right, shared, governed and safely handed over, and the answer can still be wrong. Who owns the answer? That is the next block.

Block 5 · Accountability

A computer can never be held accountable. Therefore a computer must never make a management decision.

IBM training manual, 1979.

Forty-seven years old, written about mainframes, and still the sharpest test we have. Nothing about the crowd changes it.

Let the room read it. Then ask: what is a management decision in research administration? Allowability. A compliance determination. Effort certification. A conflict-of-interest finding. The call on whether a late proposal goes out the door. Each one has a name attached to it, and that name is not the vendor's. The crowd can draft the memo. It can find the clause. It can list the precedents. It cannot be the one who signs, because when the auditor comes, "the model decided" is not an answer anyone will accept, and it should not be. This is the hard edge of augment, not replace. The deciding stays with a person not because the model is bad at it, but because accountability cannot be delegated to something that cannot be held to account.

Block 5 · Accountability

Four questions before you hand it over

1. Can you hand it everything it needs?

The crowd has read nothing of yours. If the answer depends on your award terms, your policy, or your data, they have to be in the question. If you cannot put them there, the task is "data first," not "AI now."

2. Can you check the answer cheaply?

Against a source, a ground-truth sample, a second run, or your own eyes in under a minute. If checking costs as much as doing, you have not saved anything, and you have added a risk.

3. Who is accountable for the result?

A name, a role, a signature. If the honest answer is "the AI," stop. If it is a person who will review before acting, that is augment, and it is fine.

4. Does it have to be the same tomorrow?

Audit samples, determinations, and anything a reviewer might repeat need a reproducible method. The crowd gives a different answer each time it is asked. Write the method down or use a tool that does.

Yes, yes, a named person, and a written method: use it, as augmentation. Any other answer: a person leads, or the data comes first.

This is the decision rule. Four questions, thirty seconds, before any task goes to a model. One is the context window. Two is the verification habit from the random-number slide and the whole Vandalizer block. Three is IBM. Four is the token builder: same prompt, different answer, so reproducibility has to come from the method, not the model. Run one example out loud. "Summarize the terms of this award notice." Hand it the notice: yes. Check it against the notice: yes. Accountable: the administrator who reads the summary before acting on it. Reproducible: it does not need to be, the notice is the record. Use it. Now "decide whether this cost transfer is allowable." Hand it the policy and the transaction: maybe. Check it: only by doing the analysis yourself. Accountable: a person, by regulation. Reproducible: it had better be. A person leads; the model can prepare the file. Then the continuum and the sorter make the room do this nine times.

Block 5 · Sorting the work

Does it matter how the work got done?

  • Human leads. Every step must be auditable. AI may assist, but a person owns the reasoning.
  • Human in the loop. AI prepares, a qualified person reviews and decides.
  • AI leads. Only the result matters. Validate with spot checks.

Two questions place any task. If someone asked how this was done, would the answer matter? Could a colleague re-run it and get the same result? Both "not really": AI can lead. Either "yes": a person stays in the loop.

When can AI be used in research administration?

Sequita's question, drawn as a continuum. On the left, process-critical work: the method is the product. A compliance determination is not a compliance determination if nobody can explain how it was reached. On the right, output-driven work: nobody cares how the first draft of a boilerplate paragraph got written as long as the final one is right. Most of research administration is in the middle. That is where the design question lives: what does the human see, and when?

Block 7 · Before you trust it

Three ways to check output you did not write

Ground truth

Score the extraction against documents where you already know the answers. Ten documents, hand-checked once, tell you the error rate for the next thousand.

Replication

Run it three times. Fields that agree every time are cheap confidence. Fields that shift between runs go to a person. Concordance is your routing rule.

Provenance

Every value points at a passage. "No source found" is a feature. If a claim cannot be traced to a source, it is not ready to act on.

Reading every output does not scale. Designing the check does. As tools take more steps on their own, your reading carries less of the load, and the ground truth and the trail carry more.

Go further: the four REACH evaluation experiments (OCR quality, cross-tool comparison, structured outputs, replicate consensus) with synthetic documents and ground truth are at the REACH workshop site.

Back to the Veridex question from the first hands-on. "I would read it" works for one document. Your office does not process one document. Ground truth is the bench-science move: measure the instrument before you trust it. Replication is the token-builder lesson applied: the variance is real, so use it as a signal. Provenance is the fabrication exercise applied: keep the source next to the claim. All three are things Vandalizer does, and all three are things you can do with any tool, including the chatbot your institution already licenses. The method is the point, not the product. Ground truth is also how to evaluate any tool, this one included: run twenty documents you have already processed by hand, count the misses, decide. Bridge: that is the morning. Close · Decision framework

Automate, augment, or leave alone

Automate

Repetitive, low-stakes, output-driven, with a check you can run without a person. Extraction with provenance and a ground-truth sample.

Augment

AI prepares, a qualified person decides. The budget-versus-justification review. Most of research administration lives here.

Leave alone

Process-critical work where the reasoning is the product, and anything you cannot yet audit. Until you can, it stays here.

Augment is the default. Automate only what you have measured. Leave alone what you cannot audit. Sequita's question runs under all three: can you check it?

Three bins again, now as a policy, and the cornerstone sits in the middle one. Augment is where research administration lives.
