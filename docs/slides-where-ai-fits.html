Block 1 · How LLMs work

Build a sentence one token at a time

Click the next word. Each choice shifts what comes next, exactly like an LLM.

No right answer, only probabilities. This is why the same prompt gives different output each time.

Ask for a volunteer to call out words. Build the sentence twice and get two different endings. Three things to land here. One: the model is picking the next token from a probability distribution over everything in its window. Two: the numbers above the words are attention weights, how much each earlier word matters for the next pick. Three: the bar chart is the whole space of sentences the model could have produced. Your path is one of sixty-four. Click the chart for the gradient descent sidebar if someone asks how it learned the weights. The practical consequence: run the same extraction twice and you can get different answers. That is not a bug you can configure away. It is the mechanism.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

Three ways to pick a number between 1 and 10

Random Every number equally likely. Dice, RANDBETWEEN, a sampler.
Human Skips the obvious ones, drifts toward numbers that feel random. 7 wins.
LLM Trained on humans, then tuned to be confident. The human bump becomes a spike.

The least surprising graph in the world, once you know how the model was trained.

After a chart shared by Adam Zachary Wasserman. Lesson from AI4RA Tip of the Week #25.

Hold this next to the tally you just built. Your bars are the red curve. Walk the three curves. Random is what people think they are asking for. Human is what people actually do: nobody says 1 or 10, few say 5, lots say 7. The model learned from millions of humans doing exactly that, so it inherits the bump. Then it was tuned, over and over, to give the answer people rate highest, and that tuning pushes weight toward the single most likely answer. Gradient descent rewards the peak. The bump becomes a spike. Three mechanisms to say out loud. One: it learned from people, and people are bad at random. Two: training to be confident sharpens the favorites. Three: every new chat starts from scratch, so it cannot notice it has said 7 a hundred times today; inside one chat it may avoid repeating itself and moves to the next most human answer, usually 3. Then the sentence that carries the whole morning: it is not rolling dice. It is predicting what a person would say. If someone asks how it learned: click the bar chart on the token-builder slide for the gradient descent sidebar. Same idea, same picture.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

Where "most likely" bites in research administration

When you asked for random and got "most likely"
  • "Randomly select ten awards for a spot check." The pick looks random and follows hidden patterns. That is a defensibility problem, not a statistics problem.
  • Transactions for an audit sample. Files for a quality review. Participants for a survey.
  • AI-written training quizzes put the correct answer in the same few positions, often B. Human test-writers do the same, and it learned from them.
What to do instead
  • Use a random tool when randomness matters. RANDBETWEEN, a generator, your audit software's sampler.
  • Make the model run code and show it. "Use code to randomly select 10 rows from this spreadsheet. Show me the code so I can verify the selection."
  • Check AI-written quizzes. Count which letter is correct and shuffle if it is lopsided.
  • Write down the method. An audit sample should be reproducible by someone who was not in the room.

The most likely answer is not the same thing as the right answer.

Dwell on the audit sample. A sample that was "randomly selected" by a chatbot follows the habits of every human who ever wrote the words "random sample" on the internet. If an auditor asks how it was drawn, "I asked the AI" is not a method. The fixes are cheap. A real random function is one cell in Excel. Asking the model to run code and show the code is the same move the whole morning comes back to: make the tool show its work, then verify the work instead of the vibe. That is exactly what the Vandalizer block does with document extraction. Say the callout line once more. Then the next slide generalizes it: what the model is doing, and what it is not.

Block 1 · A mental model

You are asking a crowd of a thousand non-experts

Picture a thousand well-read, well-meaning people with no particular expertise. You ask a question, and you get back whatever most of them would say, in one confident voice.

  • They have read almost everything ever published.
  • They have read none of your files unless you hand them over.
  • They answer fast, and they always answer.
  • There is no minority report. The dissenters are averaged away.
  • Ask them for a random number and you get the crowd's favorite.

How do you train a crowd? Pay some people to speak louder.

The callout is the training step. Pretraining assembles the crowd from everything ever written. Fine-tuning and reinforcement from human feedback do not teach the crowd new facts; they pay the people whose answers got good ratings to speak louder, and the rest to hush. That is why the human bump on the chart became a spike: the crowd's favorite got amplified. It is also why the voice is so confident. The loud ones were rewarded for sounding sure. This is the metaphor to carry out of the room. Not an oracle, not a search engine, not a colleague. A very large crowd of reasonably informed amateurs, polled instantly, reporting the majority view with total confidence. And it settles the cornerstone. A crowd you can consult in seconds is a tremendous thing to have beside you. It is not a replacement for the one person in the building who has read your sponsor's terms. Augment, not replace, is what the metaphor recommends on its own. Everything so far fits it. The token builder is the crowd voting on the next word. The 7 is the crowd's favorite number. The fabricated subaward, coming up, is what the crowd assumes an award summary usually says. And it tells you what to hand them. The crowd has read the whole internet and none of your award terms. If you want them to answer about your award, the award has to be in the question. That is the context window slide, two slides from now.

Block 1 · A mental model

When to ask the crowd, and when not to

Ask the crowd
  • Consensus facts
  • Summarization and synthesis
  • Common-sense reasoning
  • Brainstorming
  • Simple instruction following
  • Boilerplate and templates
  • Finding things
Do not ask the crowd
  • Niche expert knowledge
  • Verifying a novel claim
  • Ethical or moral judgment
  • Complex multi-step reasoning
  • Anything that must be reproducible

The right-hand column is shrinking. Models now run code, check their own work, and reason in steps. The left-hand column is not getting any smaller.

Left column: things a thousand reasonably informed people are genuinely good at. What does an award notice usually contain. Summarize this. Give me ten ways to phrase this. Fill in this template. Where in this document does it talk about cost share. Right column: things a crowd is bad at no matter how big. Your sponsor's unusual term. Whether a claim nobody has made before is true. What you ought to do. A twelve-step chain where one slip ruins the answer. And reproducibility: ask the crowd twice, get two answers. Say the caveat honestly. Reasoning models, tool use, and code execution are moving items from the right column leftward every year. Multi-step reasoning in particular is much better than it was. The habit to build is not "never," it is "not without checking." Bert will spend an hour on the ethical-judgment row. Research administration maps onto this cleanly: the documents go left, the determinations go right. That is the sorter exercise after the Promptulus block.

Block 1 · How LLMs work

What the model is doing, and what it is not

It is
  • Predicting the next token from everything currently in its window
  • Sampling, so the same input can produce different output
  • Drawing on patterns from an enormous training corpus
  • Very good at transforming text it has been given
It is not
  • Looking anything up unless a tool was wired in to do that
  • Remembering your last conversation, your policies, or your data
  • Checking whether what it wrote is true
  • Aware of what it does not know

Confidence is a writing style, not a signal. Every answer arrives in the same tone.

The single most useful mental model for this audience: the model only has what is in the window. Your award terms, your institutional policy, your cost-share rules are not in there unless you put them in there. When it does not have the answer, it does not stop. It produces the most plausible continuation. That is where hallucination comes from, and the next slide makes you find one.

Spot the fabrication

The AI summary. Click every sentence the source does not support.

NSF has awarded Dr. Maria Okafor $412,500 for a three-year study of soil microbial response to prescribed fire. The project includes a $60,000 subaward to the U.S. Forest Service for field site access. The award runs from September 2026 through August 2029 at an indirect rate of 48.5% MTDC. Consistent with the program's standard terms, the award requires a one-to-one institutional cost share. Annual project reports are due 90 days before the end of each budget period. Dr. Okafor will be supported by two graduate students and a postdoctoral researcher.

Give the room thirty seconds. Then reveal. Three fabrications, and they are two different kinds. The subaward and the staffing are gap-filling: the model knows award summaries usually mention these things, so it supplies plausible ones. The cost share is worse: the source says no cost share, and the model overrode it with what is typical. Priors beat evidence. The tell is that all six sentences read identically. Nothing in the prose marks which three were invented. The only way to catch them was to have the source next to it. Hold on to that: it is the whole argument for provenance in the Vandalizer block.

Block 1 · Where the data goes

Where does the model run?

Vendor-hosted (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft Copilot)

  • Access to the largest, most capable models
  • No hardware to buy or maintain
  • Pay per use, scales up and down
Security: your document leaves your network. Enterprise agreements and data protection terms matter. Some data (FERPA, HIPAA, export controlled) may not be allowed out at all. The free consumer tier is not the enterprise tier.

Self-hosted (Llama, GPT-oss, Qwen, and others)

  • Data never leaves your infrastructure
  • Full control over the model and its environment
  • Fixed cost, hardware up front
  • Ollama and vLLM make this reachable for a small IT team
Security: data stays home, but now you own the security, the hardware, and the updates. Vandalizer runs this way for restricted documents.

Cloud for most work, local for sensitive data

  • Cloud models for general drafting and high throughput
  • A desktop-class server (Mac Studio, DGX Spark) for restricted material
  • Most institutions end up here whether they planned to or not
The hard part: governance. Which models are approved for which data, and who enforces it?
Before anyone uploads a subaward agreement to a chatbot, this is the question. Where is the model, and what agreement covers the document once it gets there? Point out the practical middle: the tools we built are provider-agnostic. Vandalizer will talk to a cloud model or to a local one on the same workflow, so the policy decision and the tool decision can be separated. Transition: enough mechanism. Laptops.

Block 3 · Sorting the work

Does it matter how the work got done?

  • Human leads. Every step must be auditable. AI may assist, but a person owns the reasoning.
  • Human in the loop. AI prepares, a qualified person reviews and decides.
  • AI leads. Only the result matters. Validate with spot checks.

When can AI be used in research administration?

Sequita's question, drawn as a continuum. On the left, process-critical work: the method is the product. A compliance determination is not a compliance determination if nobody can explain how it was reached. On the right, output-driven work: nobody cares how the first draft of a boilerplate paragraph got written as long as the final one is right. Most of research administration is in the middle. That is where the design question lives: what does the human see, and when?

Block 4 · Debrief

Three ways to check output you did not write

Ground truth

Score the extraction against documents where you already know the answers. Ten documents, hand-checked once, tell you the error rate for the next thousand.

Replication

Run it three times. Fields that agree every time are cheap confidence. Fields that shift between runs go to a person. Concordance is your routing rule.

Provenance

Every value points at a passage. "No source found" is a feature. If a claim cannot be traced to a source, it is not ready to act on.

Reading every output does not scale. Designing the check does. That is what augmentation looks like at volume: the tool does the pass, you own the verification.

Go further: the four REACH evaluation experiments (OCR quality, cross-tool comparison, structured outputs, replicate consensus) with synthetic documents and ground truth are at the REACH workshop site.

Back to the Veridex question from the first hands-on. "I would read it" works for one document. Your office does not process one document. Ground truth is the bench-science move: measure the instrument before you trust it. Replication is the token-builder lesson applied: the variance is real, so use it as a signal. Provenance is the fabrication exercise applied: keep the source next to the claim. All three are things Vandalizer does, and all three are things you can do with any tool, including the chatbot your institution already licenses. The method is the point, not the product. Block 5 · The data underneath

What a data model is, and why one should be shared

You already have several

A data model is the blueprint for how a system stores information

Tables, columns, relationships, and rules. Your post-award system has one. Your ERP has another. HR has a third. They all describe the same sponsor, the same PI, and the same award differently, and every report starts by reconciling them again.

The AI4RA Universal Data Model

One shared language across institutions

Forty tables covering proposals, awards, finance, effort, compliance, and more. Map each of your systems to it once. Any tool built on the UDM then runs at any institution that has done the same.

AI makes the mapping practical: it reads your schema and the UDM and proposes the translation. A person approves it.

Browse it at ui-insight.github.io/AI4RA-UDM

The awards table failed because "department" was a free-text column somebody sometimes filled in. A data model says: Award has a required link to a Personnel record, which has a required link to an Organization. The gap becomes a validation error instead of a silent omission. The UDM is that model, shared. The reason to share it is the reason Vandalizer works at your institution without a rewrite: build once, map once, run anywhere. The adapter mapping is the one place AI is in the lakehouse pipeline itself, and it is human-approved. Every adapter works without AI too.

Block 5 · Infrastructure

Four approaches to institutional data

  • Structured, governed, fast queries
  • Pre-defined schemas enforce data quality
  • Great for reporting dashboards and compliance queries
  • Rigid schemas are expensive and slow to change
  • Cannot handle unstructured data (PDFs, images, JSON)
Best for stable reporting needs with predictable schemas.
  • Flexible raw storage, any format, cheap
  • Store everything first, decide structure later
  • Great for archiving raw files and training data
  • No schema enforcement or query optimization
  • Without governance, becomes a data swamp
Handles structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data natively.
  • Flexible storage with governance layers on top
  • Open table formats (Apache Iceberg) give transactions and schema evolution
  • Medallion architecture turns raw data into governed views
  • Storage and compute are separate, which keeps cost down
  • Works for structured, semi-structured, and unstructured data
The architecture the GRANTED project chose for university research data.
  • What happens when a data lake loses governance
  • Data dumped in with no schema, no owner, no documentation
  • Nobody knows what is in there, how old it is, or whether to trust it
  • Queries return results, but nobody can verify them
  • Every new project starts by rediscovering and recleaning the same data
The default outcome without deliberate governance. Most institutions are here, and the shared drive full of spreadsheets is the proof.
Quick tour. Warehouse: what central IT probably has, good for the dashboards it was built for, hard to change. Lake: everything in a bucket. Swamp: what a lake becomes in eighteen months. Lakehouse: a lake with the warehouse's discipline layered on top, using open formats so nothing is locked to a vendor. The point for this audience is not the technology. It is that "AI-ready" is a governance property. If your office's reporting runs off a shared drive of spreadsheets, you are in the swamp panel, and no model fixes that.

Block 5 · Architecture

The medallion layers

Bronze

Raw data, exactly as received. No transformation.

→
Silver

Per-source mapping to the UDM. One view per source per table.

→
Gold

Unified UDM tables. Sources combined, deduplicated by primary key.

→
Platinum

App-specific views. Joins, aggregations, access control.

Store everything. Raw files, soft deletes, metadata. The audit trail starts here.

Translate once per source. AI proposes the column mapping, a person approves it. No business logic, only standardization.

One truth. If two sources describe the same award, priority rules pick the winner. Completeness checks live here.

Purpose-built. Each dashboard, report, or AI agent gets exactly the view it needs, and nothing it should not see.

If the model reads from Gold instead of a spreadsheet, the missing departments are flagged before the question is ever asked.

Back to the awards table. Bronze keeps the table as it arrived, blanks and all, so you can always prove what the source said. Silver maps "Department" to the UDM's Organization link. Gold is where the completeness rule runs: an Award without an Organization does not pass. The VP's question gets answered from Gold, or it gets a flag that says the answer is incomplete and by how much. That is the difference between a tool that is confidently wrong and a pipeline that is honestly incomplete.

The GRANTED data lakehouse

Data flow Bronze Silver Platinum Live Writeback

Open source, Docker-managed, built for a small team. Every layer is replaceable: MinIO or S3, Trino or DuckDB, our sync engine or Airbyte. github.com/ui-insight/lakehouse

Left to right: your source systems, the adapters that translate them to the shared schema, the lakehouse itself, an API gateway that enforces who can see what, and the apps on the end. The admin portal on top is where a small team configures all of it. The shared schema feeding the adapters is the UDM. Only the gateway is exposed. Block 5 · The payoff

What well-organized data lets you do

With governed data
  • Ask the same question at any institution and get a comparable answer
  • Build AI workflows that cite their sources and can be audited
  • Generate reproducible reports that match across departments
  • Move tasks from the "data first" bin into the "AI now" bin
Without it
  • AI blends contradictory sources into confident wrong answers
  • Every new report requires a new integration from scratch
  • Nobody knows which spreadsheet is current or authoritative
  • Automation multiplies errors instead of eliminating them
The last bullet on the left is the whole block in one line. The sorter had three tasks stuck in "data first." Governed data is what moves them. It is not that AI cannot answer the VP's question. It is that the question has to be answerable first.

Close · Decision framework

Automate, augment, or leave alone

Automate

Repetitive, low-stakes, output-driven, with a check you can run without a person. Extraction with provenance and a ground-truth sample.

Augment

AI prepares, a qualified person decides. The budget-versus-justification review. Most of research administration lives here.

Leave alone

Process-critical work where the reasoning is the product, and anything you cannot yet audit. Until you can, it stays here.

Augment is the default. Automate only what you have measured. Leave alone what you cannot audit. Sequita's question runs under all three: can you check it?

Three bins again, now as a policy, and the cornerstone sits in the middle one. Augment is where research administration lives. Automate is earned, by measuring a workflow until you trust the check more than you trust your own reading. Leave alone is honest, not timid. The sorter's "data first" tasks are "leave alone" until the data exists, then they move. The human tasks stay human. The document tasks are augment today and automate once you have measured them.
