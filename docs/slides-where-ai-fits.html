What is AI

What we mean by AI

When people say "AI" these days, they usually mean that little LLM circle. And all an LLM does is predict the next word.

Point at the person on the ladder first. Call it Simon's ladder and do not explain the name; that is what the click is for. We are building a ladder to understanding AI, without the jargon. Mental models are rungs on a ladder. Each one is wrong in some way, but each lets you see further than the one below. You don't need to reach the top; you need to reach the rung that lets you see what you're working on. Hover over him and the same line shows on screen. Easter egg: clicking him opens Herbert Simon's Wikipedia page in a new tab. If someone asks who Simon was: the Nobel economist who coined "satisficing," stopping at the answer good enough to act on rather than the perfect one. Thirty seconds on the circles, outside in. Artificial intelligence is the big umbrella: any computer system doing a task we would otherwise call intelligent, and most of it is old and boring: spell check, spam filters, the fraud alert on your card. Machine learning is the part that learns the pattern from examples instead of being given the rules. Generative AI is machine learning that makes new content, text, images, audio, instead of only sorting or scoring. Large language models are generative AI trained on an enormous amount of text to do one thing, predict the next word, and they are what everyone means by AI right now: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot. Point at the orange oval: natural language processing is the older field of getting computers to work with human language, and much of it is not machine learning at all, rules and dictionaries. LLMs sit where it overlaps the rest, which is why they feel so different from earlier language tools. If anyone asks how the people who regulate and fund this define it: the National AI Initiative Act (15 U.S.C. 9401) calls it a machine-based system that makes predictions, recommendations or decisions for human-defined objectives. NIH's data science plan calls it the power of a machine to copy intelligent human behavior. University guidelines typically say a system that performs tasks that typically require human intelligence. Notice that all three define it by what it does. None says where its knowledge comes from. Hold that; it comes back in block 3. Then: the one job is predict the next word. What is AI · A mental model

You are asking a crowd of a thousand non-experts

Picture a thousand well-read, well-meaning people with no particular expertise. You ask a question, and you get back whatever most of them would say, in one confident voice.

  • They have read almost everything ever published.
  • They have read none of your files unless you hand them over.
  • They answer fast, and they always answer.
  • There is no minority report. The dissenters are averaged away.
  • Ask them for a random number and see what you get.

How do you train a crowd? Pay some people to speak louder.

The callout is the training step. Pretraining assembles the crowd from everything ever written. Fine-tuning and reinforcement from human feedback do not teach the crowd new facts; they pay the people whose answers got good ratings to speak louder, and the rest to hush. That is also why the voice is so confident. The loud ones were rewarded for sounding sure. This is the metaphor to carry out of the room. Not an oracle, not a search engine, not a colleague. A very large crowd of reasonably informed amateurs, polled instantly, reporting the majority view with total confidence. And it settles the cornerstone. A crowd you can consult in seconds is a tremendous thing to have beside you. It is not a replacement for the one person in the building who has read your sponsor's terms. Augment, not replace, is what the metaphor recommends on its own. Everything after this fits it. Watch for the crowd in the pachinko board, where it votes on the next word, and in the random number game. And it tells you what to hand them. The crowd has read the whole internet and none of your award terms. If you want them to answer about your award, the award has to be in the question. That is Block 2.

From nails to a trained model

1. The nails are weights, in layers

A real model is a neural network. Your words go in on the left, pass through layer after layer, and come out on the right as odds for the next word. Every line is a number, a weight, that decides how much one node passes to the next. The board has a few dozen nails; a large language model has billions of weights.

2. Wrong answers make a landscape

Training feeds in a sentence people wrote, hides the next word, and measures how wrong the guess was. Every possible setting of the weights has an error score. Picture those scores as hills and valleys: high ground is a bad guess, the valley floor is a good one. Training is the walk downhill.

3. How do we know which way to move the nails?

Feel for the slope. Nudge a weight a tiny bit: does the error go up or down? With billions of weights, testing them one at a time would take forever, so we use a fancy maths trick called gradient descent. It works out the downhill direction for every weight at once, takes a small step, and measures again. Each arrow on the map is one step toward the low point.

The result lands where people’s words usually land. It learned what is likely to be written, not what is true.

Ask the crowd
  • Consensus facts. What an award notice usually contains.

Three ways to pick a number between 1 and 10

Random Every number equally likely. Dice, RANDBETWEEN, a sampler.
Human Skips the obvious ones, drifts toward numbers that feel random. 7 wins.
LLM Trained on humans, then tuned to be confident. The human bump becomes a spike.

The least surprising graph in the world, once you know how the model was trained.

After a chart shared by Adam Zachary Wasserman. Lesson from AI4RA Tip of the Week #25.

Hold this next to the tally you just built. Your bars are the red curve. Walk the three curves. Random is what people think they are asking for. Human is what people actually do: nobody says 1 or 10, few say 5, lots say 7. The model learned from millions of humans doing exactly that, so it inherits the bump. Then it was tuned, over and over, to give the answer people rate highest, and that tuning pushes weight toward the single most likely answer. Gradient descent rewards the peak. The bump becomes a spike. Three mechanisms to say out loud. One: it learned from people, and people are bad at random. Two: training to be confident sharpens the favorites. Three: every new chat starts from scratch, so it cannot notice it has said 7 a hundred times today; inside one chat it may avoid repeating itself and moves to the next most human answer, usually 3. Then the sentence that carries the whole morning: it is not rolling dice. It is predicting what a person would say. Name it: the 7 is a bias. It came from the people who wrote the training text, and tuning made it stronger. Here it is harmless. Hold that thought; the mirror slide comes back to it after the next one. The problems with AI

What does this mean for a research administrator?

When you asked for random but got random-sounding
  • "Randomly select ten awards for a spot check." The pick looks random and follows hidden patterns. That is a defensibility problem, not a statistics problem.
  • Transactions for an audit sample. Files for a quality review. Participants for a survey.
  • AI-written training quizzes put the correct answer in the same few positions, often B. Human test-writers do the same, and it learned from them.
What to do instead
  • Use a random tool when randomness matters. RANDBETWEEN, a generator, your audit software's sampler.
  • Make the model run code and show it. "Use code to randomly select 10 rows from this spreadsheet. Show me the code so I can verify the selection."
  • Check AI-written quizzes. Count which letter is correct and shuffle if it is lopsided.
  • Write down the method. An audit sample should be reproducible by someone who was not in the room.

The most likely random-sounding answer is not the same as an answer that is actually random.

Dwell on the audit sample. A sample that was "randomly selected" by a chatbot follows the habits of every human who ever wrote the words "random sample" on the internet. If an auditor asks how it was drawn, "I asked the AI" is not a method. The fixes are cheap. A real random function is one cell in Excel. Asking the model to run code and show the code is the same move the whole morning comes back to: make the tool show its work, then verify the work instead of the vibe. That is exactly what the Vandalizer block does with document extraction. Say the callout line once more. Then the next slide names what the 7 really was: a bias.

The problems with AI

AI is a mirror, and we are biased

AI is biased, because it learns from us and we are biased.

An AI reflects and amplifies our inherent bias. It learned the 7 from us, and tuning reinforced that bias. And it was created for biased reasons: its makers chose the data and the tuning to serve their own interests.

Bonus: go watch Hans Rosling’s The best stats you’ve ever seen (TED, 2006) if you want to learn about the difference between how we think about the world and how it really is.

This is the point under the random-number game, so give it room. The 7 was a bias: the model learned it from the people who wrote its training text, and tuning made it stronger. It is a mirror that reflects and amplifies our inherent bias. We are biased, so it will be too, and there is no unbiased version coming. And it is not only us in the mirror. The companies that build these models choose the training data and decide how the model is tuned, and those choices serve their interests. The Grok aside on the model quiz shows what that looks like. The problems with AI · Memory and the record

An ancient tale, written down by Plato 2,400 years ago

Set in the distant past of Egypt. When the god Theuth offered writing to King Thamus, the king replied:

This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners’ souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves. The specific which you have discovered is an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence, and you give your disciples not truth, but only the semblance of truth; they will be hearers of many things and will have learned nothing; they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing; they will be tiresome company, having the show of wisdom without the reality.

Thamus thought writing would weaken people's memory, and that leaning on reminders would weaken their reasoning. But writing something down can help you remember it later, and, most importantly, when you look up a fact that has been written down, you can be confident it hasn't changed.

Plato, Phaedrus 274c to 275b, written around 370 BCE. Benjamin Jowett’s translation.

Two minutes, told, not read. Socrates tells it to Phaedrus as an old Egyptian tale he once heard, set at Naucratis in a mythic past when gods offered their inventions to kings. It has no date, and most scholars think Plato invented it; what is datable is the writing, around 370 BCE. Theuth brings his inventions to the king and argues for each; writing is the one he is proudest of. Thamus answers that the inventor is the last person to judge what an invention does to the people who use it, and then gives the verdict on the slide. Land on the distinction: memory versus reminding. He meant reminding as the lesser thing. Then turn it on the machine in one breath: a model's memory is its training, enormous, confident, frozen on a date, stale after it. The fix for a machine is the reminding the king refused. Four ways an answer goes wrong

“Hallucination” is a misleading name. Psychology has a better one: confabulation, a reconstructive memory error, where the mind fills a gap with something plausible and believes it. People do it too.

Missed it

A false negative

Something in the source never made it into the answer.

A prior-approval requirement left off the award setup.

Made it up

A false positive

Something appears that the source never said.

A subaward, or a citation, that does not exist.

Changed a detail

A distortion

The right item, with one piece quietly altered.

15 percent becomes 10; an end date slips a year; “may” becomes “must.”

A misattribution

A real fact, attached to the wrong field, person or source.

The co-PI listed as the PI; a clause quoted from the wrong section.

Using AI responsibly · The decision rule

Five questions before AI helps with a task

1. Can you give the model everything it needs?

The crowd has read nothing of yours. If the answer depends on your award terms, your policy, or your data, they have to be in the question. If you cannot put them there, the task is "data first," not "AI now."

2. Can you check the answer cheaply?

Against a source, a ground-truth sample, a second run, or your own eyes in under a minute. If checking costs as much as doing, you have not saved anything, and you have added a risk.

3. Who is accountable for the result?

A name, a role, a signature. If the honest answer is "the AI," stop. If it is a person who will review before acting, that is augment, and it is fine.

4. Does it have to be the same tomorrow?

Audit samples, determinations, and anything a reviewer might repeat need a reproducible method. The crowd gives a different answer each time it is asked. Write the method down or use a tool that does.

5. Is it allowed?

Your sponsor’s AI rules and your institution’s guidance come first. If the answer here is no, the other four do not matter.

If the answers are yes, yes, a named person, a written method, and the rules allow it, let AI help. If not, a person does the work, or the data gets fixed first.

This is the decision rule. Five questions, thirty seconds, before you bring a model into any task. One is the context window. Vendor-hosted (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft Copilot)

  • The largest, most capable models, with no hardware to buy or maintain
  • Pay per use, scales with the funding cycle
  • Your document leaves the building, and so does the computing. You do not see where it ran or what it cost in energy.
Two tiers. Public text you could post on a bulletin board: a personal key on a free tier is fine, and that is all it is for. Self-hosted (Llama, GPT-oss, Qwen, and others, through Ollama or vLLM)

  • Data never leaves your house. FERPA, HIPAA, PII, and export-controlled material stay where policy says they stay.
  • You know exactly where the computing is done, by whom, and on what hardware.
  • You know how much energy you are using. A desktop-class server runs a day of extraction for a few kilowatt-hours; cloud inference is bundled and invisible.
  • Fixed cost, up front. Vandalizer and MindRouter run this way at Idaho.
The trade: you own the security, the hardware, and the updates. Smaller models than the cloud frontier, though the gap closes every year.

  • Enterprise cloud for drafting, summarizing public documents, and high throughput
  • A local server for award files, personnel data, and anything with a regulation attached
  • Where most institutions land, whether they planned to or not

Using AI responsibly · Your institution’s AI guidance

How strict should it be?

1 Principles General values; AI mentioned in passing, if at all. “Our use of AI will keep human judgment at the center.”
2 Advisory Best practices and a few firm rules. “Err on the side of disclosing AI use, and verify all outputs.”
3 Explicit expectations Specific data and disclosure rules, mostly self-enforced. “No confidential data in AI tools; disclose AI use above 25% of a deliverable.”
4 Controlled use Approved tools by data risk level, and a review process for new ones. “Moderate-risk data only in tools approved for that level.”
5 Strict Approved tools on approved infrastructure only, with required disclaimers and enforcement. “One approved tool; every output carries an AI disclaimer.”

Choose the level first, and bring IT and general counsel into that decision. Most other choices follow from it.

Five levels, from a statement of values to a single approved tool with a required disclaimer. None is right for everyone: a campus with strict security compliance, tight data storage rules or heavy audit exposure will sit higher. The policies in the review also split on a handful of design choices, worth naming quickly if there is time: principles versus requirements; naming approved tools versus describing what kinds are allowed; when disclosure is required, from any material use to a percentage threshold to merely encouraged; one office versus the whole institution; and a standalone AI policy versus clauses folded into data governance. The safest default on disclosure is to always disclose. The point for the room: these are choices to make on purpose and write down, early, with IT and general counsel.
