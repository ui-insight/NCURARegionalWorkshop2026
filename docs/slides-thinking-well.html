How a NARC puzzle works

1. A strip of small grids

Three to eight frames that show something happening. One frame is hidden.

2. A short story

It never mentions grids, cells, or colors. It helps only if you read it as being about the shapes.

3. Rebuild the hidden grid

Exactly, every cell the right color. Close does not count.

4. Test each model three ways

Grids only, story only, and both together.

Part 2 · How Narrate works

You see the answer. The model doesn’t.

→ → →
1

You see every grid, including the one the model won’t see.

2

You write a story about what happens. No coordinates, no spelling out cells.

3

The model gets the other grids and your story, and has to rebuild the hidden one exactly.

4

You see whether it worked. Revise and try again.

Narrate turns the puzzle around. You see all the grids, including the one the model won't see. You know the answer. Your job is to write a story about what happens, so that the model can rebuild the hidden grid from the other grids and your story. You're not allowed to spell out cells or coordinates. It has to be a story. You press a button, the model tries, and you see whether it worked. Then you revise. This is close to a lot of AI use at work. You know what the right answer is, or at least you'd know it if you saw it. The question is how to describe the situation so the tool gets there.

Part 3 · Ethics and arguments by analogy

Where do ethical claims come from?

A new technology has no settled cases yet. So we borrow them.

Now ethics. I'm going to give you one tool, the argument by analogy. It comes from the AI ethics guide we hand out at our workshops. The reason it matters for AI is that AI is new. There are no settled cases about it yet. So when people argue about it, they borrow cases from somewhere else.

Part 3 · Ethics and arguments by analogy

Two kinds of claims

What is the case

“Most of our office now uses AI to draft routine emails.”

You check it by looking.

What ought to be the case

“We ought to disclose AI use in routine emails.”

You check it by reasoning.

“Everyone is doing it” does not make it fine. You can’t get an ought from an is alone.

First, a distinction. Some claims are about what is the case. Others are about what ought to be the case. You check the first kind by looking. Count the people in your office who use AI for email. You check the second kind by reasoning. A common slide in office conversation goes from one to the other: everyone is using it without saying so, so it must be fine. That doesn't work. Philosophers call it the naturalistic fallacy. So if you can't get an ought from the facts alone, where do ethical claims come from? You start from cases where people already agree, and you reason outward. The most common way to do that is the argument by analogy.

Part 3 · Ethics and arguments by analogy

The argument by analogy

X

In a settled case X, doing A is required (or not allowed).

Y

The new case Y is like X in the ways that matter.

So

In case Y, doing A is also required (or not allowed).

To push back: find a relevant difference between X and Y. What counts as relevant is something you work out together.

Here is the form. You have a settled case, X, where people agree about what you should do. You claim the new case, Y, is like X in the ways that matter. So the same verdict carries over. [click] The way to push back is to find a difference between the two cases. But not just any difference. It has to be a difference that matters for the verdict. And what counts as relevant isn't given by the analogy. You find it out by arguing about it, which is why this works best in conversation.

Part 3 · Ethics and arguments by analogy

A worked example

“A new AI tool is like a new medication. We test medications for safety and regulate them before use. So AI tools in high-stakes settings should get safety testing and oversight too.”

“Medications go inside your body. AI tools don’t.”

A real difference. But does it bear on whether we need safety checks? Probably not.

“AI tools change with every update. A drug’s formula stays fixed.”

This one matters. One-time approval assumes the thing you approved stays the same.

“AI tools get used in ways their makers never planned.”

Does this mean more oversight, or a different kind? Now you are arguing about what is relevant.

Three proposed differences. Click each: does it matter for the conclusion?

Here's an example from the guide. A new AI tool is like a new medication. We test medications for safety and regulate them. So we should do the same for AI tools. Here are three proposed differences. Let's take them one at a time. Ask the room before you click. First: medications go inside your body. That's a difference. But it doesn't obviously bear on whether we need safety checks. So it's probably not relevant. Second: AI tools change with every update. A drug's formula doesn't. That one matters, because the whole approval system assumes the thing you approved stays the same. Third: AI tools get used in ways their makers never planned. Does that mean we need more oversight, or a different kind? This is where the real argument happens.

Part 3 · Your turn, 3 minutes with a neighbor

Find the relevant difference

“Using AI to draft PI communications is like using a paid copyeditor. We don’t normally disclose that we used a copyeditor. So we don’t need to disclose AI use.”

“Using AI to draft a routine response email is like using a form letter library. We’ve used form letters for decades without disclosing them. So AI use in routine emails doesn’t need disclosure.”

Ask: Same level of judgment? Same accuracy? Same person answering for it?

If you can’t find one, that is a finding too. The analogy may hold.

Your turn. Two analogies I've heard in research administration conversations, both about disclosing AI use. Pick one with your neighbor. You have three minutes. Find a difference between the two cases that matters for the conclusion. Or, if you can't, defend the analogy. That's a legitimate result. It may mean disclosure isn't required. Part 3 · Ethics and arguments by analogy

Analogies you will hear about AI

“It’s a calculator.”

Do you check a calculator’s arithmetic? What would make you check the AI’s?

“It’s an intern.”

An intern can tell you when they’re unsure, and learns your office over time. Does the AI?

“It’s autocomplete.”

A good description of how it works. Does it tell you when to trust the output?

“It’s a copyeditor.”

A copyeditor answers to someone. Who answers for the AI’s output? You do.

Each one is an argument by analogy. Where does it fit, and where does it break?

Every picture people use for AI is an analogy, and each one carries a verdict with it. If it's a calculator, you don't need to check it. If it's an intern, you supervise it. If it's autocomplete, you treat it as a guess. Quick round. For each one, where does it fit and where does it break? Click after the room has had a go. The last one is the one I care about most for this audience. The accountability stays with you, not the model. That's true whichever analogy you prefer. Keep this short. Two minutes.

Part 4 · A workshop in miniature

Take a position before you have a framework

A short version of the dialogue workshops we ran with research administrators in spring 2026.

In spring 2026 we ran small-group dialogue workshops with research administrators about AI. Each one was built around a set of statements that people respond to and then discuss. I'm going to show you how the statements are built, and then we'll run two of them. The goal is that you could run this at your own institution next month.

Part 4 · How the instrument is built

Built to start an argument

Statements, not questions

You react to a claim, so you have to take a side.

Strong words on purpose

“Always,” “must,” and “never” give people something to push against.

“I Don’t Know” is an answer

A pile of them points to a gap in information, not a lack of interest.

Four groups of statements

Know-how, values and risk, what “AI literacy” means, and wider costs. They help separate why people hesitate.

Run by a neutral facilitator. Voluntary and anonymous. Anyone can pass.

The instrument has sixteen statements. A few design choices make it work. They're statements, not questions. You react to a claim, so you have to take a side. They use strong words on purpose. Always, must, never. A mild statement gets mild agreement and nothing to talk about. A strong one splits the room. "I Don't Know" is a real option, and we pay attention to it. When a lot of people pick it, that tells you people are missing information. It's not that they don't care. And the statements come in four groups: technical know-how, ethical values and risk, what "AI literacy" means, and wider economic and environmental costs. The groups help you tell apart the reasons people hesitate. Someone who doesn't feel confident with the tool is in a different place from someone who thinks the task shouldn't be automated at all. Each session had a neutral facilitator. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and anyone could pass.

Part 4 · Round 1

Where do you stand?

If an administrator cannot verify the output of an AI tool manually, they should never use that tool for grant submissions.

Statement 2 of 16 · Technical competency and methodology

Read the statement out loud. Give everyone fifteen seconds to decide, privately, before anyone says anything. That part matters. Positions first, discussion second. Then on three, hands up: one finger for disagree up to five for agree. A fist means "I don't know." Hand down means pass. If the room is small, click the buttons to tally the hands so everyone can see the spread. If it's big, just describe what you see: "Lots of fours, a few ones, three fists." Then ask someone from each side to say why, in one or two sentences. Don't argue yet. Next slide.

Part 4 · Round 1, discussion

Where does the disagreement live?

Facts

Is it even possible to check this output by hand, at our volume?

You settle it by looking.

Values

Is a shortcut you can’t check ever worth the risk to a submission?

You settle it by reasoning, and analogies help.

Words

What counts as “verify”? A spot check? Every line? What does “manually” rule out?

You settle it by agreeing on terms.

Then ask each side: what would change your mind?

When people disagree, the disagreement usually lives in one of three places. Sometimes it's about facts: can anyone actually check this output by hand, given how much we process? Sometimes it's about values: is an unverifiable shortcut ever worth the risk to a submission? And sometimes it's about words: what does "verify" mean? A spot check? Every line? Ask the room which one their disagreement is. Often two people who "disagree" turn out to mean different things by "verify." Notice that "never" and "manually" are doing a lot of work in this statement. That's on purpose. [click] Then ask each side what would change their mind. If someone can't name anything, their position hasn't been tested yet. That's not a criticism. It's where the next conversation starts. About five minutes total for this round.

Part 4 · Round 2

Now bring an analogy

It is unethical to utilize AI for drafting internal communications without explicitly disclosing it to the recipients.

Statement 7 of 16 · Ethical values and risk

Which old case is this like? A copyeditor? A form letter? Spell-check? Find the relevant difference.

Same routine. Read it, fifteen seconds of private thought, then hands. This is the disclosure question from Part 3, stated strongly. So this time, after the vote, ask people to defend their answer with an analogy. [click] Someone who disagrees might say it's like a copyeditor or a form letter. Someone who agrees has to find the relevant difference. And someone will usually point out that "always disclose" proves too much: do you disclose spell-check? Then the room has to say where the line is, and why. That's the whole method in about five minutes: take a stance, see the room, find where the disagreement lives, and reason with cases.
