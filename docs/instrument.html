Research Administration and AI: A Discussion Instrument

Sixteen statements about AI in research administration, with a guide for running a facilitated conversation about them. Free to use and adapt under a Creative Commons license.

Download PDF Download Word (editable)

What this is

Built to start a conversation

The instrument is a set of sixteen statements. Participants say how much they agree with each one, and then a facilitator leads a discussion of where the group agrees and where it splits. It is not a survey, and it is not meant to measure attitudes.

The statements use strong words on purpose: “always,” “must,” “never.” A mild statement gets mild agreement and leaves nothing to talk about. A strong one makes people take a side, and when they explain why, you learn what they actually care about. The statements come in four modules, which help separate the reasons people hesitate about AI: know-how, values and risk, what “AI literacy” means, and wider economic and environmental costs.

We wrote the instrument for dialogue workshops with research administrators in spring 2026. This version was prepared for participants in the AI4RA workshop at the NCURA Region VI/VII Regional Meeting in Denver, November 2026.

Who should facilitate

Free to use. Best with a trained facilitator.

The instrument is free. Ideally, the person who facilitates has training in ethics, philosophy, or a related field. The statements are designed to provoke, and a trained facilitator keeps the disagreement useful: they help the group tell apart disagreements about facts, about values, and about what a word means, and they test arguments without steering the room toward their own view.

If no one in your office has that background, a colleague from a philosophy or ethics program on your campus may be glad to help. Either way, the facilitator should not be anyone’s supervisor.

Facilitator guide

How to run a session

Before

  • Choose your statements. All sixteen take about 10 to 15 minutes to answer. For a shorter session, use one module, or three or four statements.
  • You can write your own statements about a decision your office faces. Keep the strong words.
  • Find a neutral facilitator. Small groups work best.

During

  • Participation is voluntary. Anyone can pass on any statement.
  • Everyone responds privately before anyone speaks. Use the sheet, a show of hands (one to five fingers, a fist for “I don’t know”), or an anonymous poll.
  • Show the group’s spread, then discuss. Keep responses anonymous.
  • “I Don’t Know” and “N/A” are real answers. Count them.

After

  • Write down the reasoning, not just the result. A future colleague will need to know why you decided what you did.
  • Many “I Don’t Know” answers on one statement usually point to information your institution could supply.
  • Run it again later. Positions change as the tools and policies do.

Questions for the discussion

  • Where does the disagreement live? In the facts (what is the case), in values (what ought to be the case), or in words (what a key term means)?
  • Which word in the statement is doing the work? Try the statement without “always” or “never.” Does your answer change?
  • What would change your mind? If you can’t name anything, the position hasn’t been tested yet.
  • Is someone arguing by analogy? (“Using AI here is like using a form letter.”) Look for a relevant difference: a difference between the two cases that matters for the conclusion.
  • Where did your own answer surprise you? Where did the group’s?

Facilitator guide

What each module is for

Module A · Statements 1 to 4

Technical competency and methodology

Finds out whether hesitancy comes from a lack of technical confidence or know-how. Many “I Don’t Know” answers here point to a literacy gap.

To what extent does effective research administration require technical proficiency in AI?

Module B · Statements 5 to 8

Ethical values and risk

Finds out whether hesitancy comes from moral obligation or from risk aversion. These statements force a trade-off between efficiency and correctness.

Do ethical risks outweigh the efficiency gains of AI in research administration?

Module C · Statements 9 to 11

Defining “AI literacy”

Works on a messy concept. The statements are ambiguous on purpose, so the group has to negotiate a shared definition.

What constitutes “literacy” in the context of automated administration?

Module D · Statements 12 to 16

Economic and environmental impact

Finds out whether hesitancy comes from wider concerns, such as job security or climate change, rather than day-to-day workflow or skills. It separates a “local” worry (I can’t do this task) from a “global” one (this task shouldn’t exist).

To what extent should global economic and environmental factors influence local administrative decisions?

Module D often draws “I Don’t Know.” These resources ground the environmental conversation in real data: what-uses-more.com (energy and water costs of everyday digital activities), your-digital-life.org (the footprint of your own digital habits), and the University of Idaho’s GPU telemetry analysis (the footprint of one campus AI cluster).
