# Where AI fits in research administration: the argument of Part 1

The first ninety minutes of a three-hour NCURA Region VI/VII workshop (Denver, Sunday, November 8, 2026), led by Nathan Layman, a data scientist on AI4RA, an NSF-funded University of Idaho project (award 2427549) that builds open-source AI tools for research administration offices. After a break, Bert Baumgaertner, a philosopher at the same university, leads an hour on thinking well with AI, and the day closes with half an hour of open discussion.

**How to read this.** This is the argument the talk makes, in the order it makes it, written for a research administrator who will be in the seat. It is a concept piece, not a manual. Judge it on whether the story holds: Where does it lose you? What would you need to hear that is not here? Is any block in the wrong place? And when it ends, do you know what you would do differently, and why? The companion site at https://ui-insight.github.io/NCURARegionalWorkshop2026/ holds the slides, the exercise sheets, and the recipes; every rule, question, and checklist the argument depends on is reproduced here; the slides, the tools, and the UDM itself are not, and are linked. One caution that the talk itself makes about every written record applies to this one: it was written in October 2026. Regulation figures, tool names, prices, and account terms can all have moved by the time you read it. The eCFR, the companion site, and the tools' own pages are live; this page is not.

## The spine

One sentence runs through all ninety minutes: **augment, not replace. AI prepares, a person decides.** Each block adds one reason that sentence is true and one skill for living by it.

- **Opening.** The one sentence, the paste rule, and the disclosure.
1. The model guesses the most likely next word, so it is a crowd, not an expert. Skill: know what to ask a crowd.
2. The crowd answers better when you tell it what you want. Skill: prompt with intent, hands-on.
3. A king who distrusted the written record, and the crowd's memory is stale, so give it the record. Skill: recognize recalled versus accessed, and ask what was left out.
4. The record has to be right, shared, and governed, and handing it over has a cost. Skill: know where your data goes.
5. Only a person can be accountable. Skill: four questions and a sort.
6. Start with what is on your desk. Skill: three small things this month.
7. A reusable workflow can be checked once and trusted many times. Skill: three ways to check output you did not write.
- **Close.** Why we still know the king's name, and which half of his warning still stands.

Ninety minutes in all: an opening of 3, blocks of 15, 15, 16, 10, 9, 7 and 11, and a close of 4.

## Opening (3 minutes)

The spine sentence, said once and plainly. A disclosure in the same breath: the tools in this talk are open source, free, and built by the presenters' project on an NSF award, so judge them by the checks the talk teaches, not by who is showing them. And one rule for the hands-on block before it starts: paste only what you could post on a bulletin board. PI names, sponsor names, award titles, deadlines, and public solicitation text are fine. Salaries, student or patient information, export-controlled detail, anything a sponsor marks confidential, and the content of unfunded proposals, the abstracts, aims, and narratives that are the PI's confidential work, are not. At the edges, the sponsor's terms and your institution's data classification decide, and proposal content is the PI's call. The full account of where data goes comes in the data block, deliberately, after the room has handed a model something and felt the question.

## Block 1. How language models work (15 minutes)

A language model builds a sentence one word at a time by picking the most likely next word. Two exercises make that visible: the room builds a sentence together, one word per person, and everyone picks a random number between 1 and 10, which shows that a model's picks cluster the way a room's do. The mental model to carry: asking an AI is like asking a crowd of a thousand well-read non-experts. Excellent on consensus knowledge, unreliable on anything specialized, local, or recent. A spot-the-fabrication exercise shows what "most likely" does to a citation. One practical limit closes the block: the model can only read so many pages at once, somewhere between a long report and a short book depending on the tool, which matters the moment you start handing it documents.

**Hand-off:** if the model is a crowd guessing what you probably mean, the first skill is telling it what you actually mean.

## Block 2. Promptulus, hands-on (15 minutes)

Everyone opens Promptulus, a prompt-training page, picks a coach (your choice; Telosa, the intent coach, is the right first pick for almost any task), and works a real task from their desk. What you type, and the task text you paste, go to the model your key points at and nowhere else; the page itself stores nothing. Where it goes from there is governed by whoever runs the model: the university gateway in the room logs each request, which is what an audit trail is, and a free consumer tier runs under the vendor's terms, which can include using your inputs to train. That is the whole reason the free key is for public text only. Each coach is named for one habit: Telosa for intent, Mnemos for context, Modulus for breaking a task into steps, Sequita for an auditable trail, Dialogos for conversation, Vitrea for transparency, Veridex for evaluation, Clarion for reporting. The coach asks what you want, who it is for, and what a good answer would contain before the model writes a word. Four minutes to get everyone in, eight to work, three to debrief on one question: what did the coach make you say that you had not thought to say?

What an exchange looks like. You type: "Summarize this award notice." Telosa answers with questions, not a summary: Who reads the summary, and what will they do with it? Which terms would be expensive to miss, cost share, reporting dates, prior-approval requirements? How long should it be? You answer, and the prompt that finally goes to the model is three sentences longer and gets a summary a post-award officer can act on. The habit is the point, not the tool: the three questions work typed into any chat window.

**Hand-off:** a well-told prompt still gets a wrong answer when the crowd's memory is wrong. The coach reminded you what to say; nothing yet has reminded the model what is true. The next block begins with a very old argument about exactly this.

## Block 3. Reminding the machine (16 minutes)

The story first, told in two minutes. In Plato's *Phaedrus*, the god Theuth invents writing and presents it to King Thamus as a remedy for memory. Thamus disagrees: people will trust the marks on the page and stop remembering for themselves. Writing, he says, is a remedy not for memory but for reminding. He meant that as the flaw. Then the story turns onto the machine. A model's memory is its training: enormous, confident, frozen on a date, stale after it. So the fix for a machine is exactly the reminding Thamus refused, and the page named for him is where the room watches it.

**Demo one, five answers to one question, on Remembering Thamus.** What de minimis indirect cost rate may a recipient without a negotiated rate charge?

- From memory, the model says 10 percent. That was the rule in most of what it read. It is wrong today.
- From a pasted passage, the answer is as good as the paste, and the page can fill in the 2023 text to prove it.
- From a web search, it finds a blog post as happily as the rule.
- From a search index of the regulation, the approach most products call retrieval-augmented generation, the passages that look most similar to the question are handed to the model. It gets close, but the passage it leaned on may be the wrong one, and the answer blends what it found with what it remembered. The rule the room takes away: an index is fine for finding and summarizing your own documents; anything you will cite, and anything that changes on a schedule, like a rate or a regulation, wants the live lookup.
- From a live lookup, it searches the eCFR, reads one paragraph, and answers 15 percent of modified total direct costs (MTDC, the base most indirect cost rates are charged against), citing 2 CFR 200.414(f) as in force on the day of the lookup. Anyone can check it at https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.414, and anyone should, because this page is a frozen record too: it states the rule as of the day it was written, and if the eCFR says something else on the day you read this, the eCFR is right and the page has just demonstrated its own point. The rate changed on October 1, 2024, with the revised Uniform Guidance; the equipment threshold ($5,000 to $10,000) and the subaward portion counted toward MTDC ($25,000 to $50,000) changed the same day and make good second questions.

Watch one thing: the model sounds equally sure in all three columns. Confidence is not evidence. (Each live demo has a fallback on the slide: a captured run with the same citation and the same eCFR link, so a dead network slows the lesson without turning it into "trust me.")

**Demo two, the same lesson on a spreadsheet.** Data Crawler Carl is asked for total federal funding by department on a small awards table. It writes a correct query that silently drops every award whose department was left blank, and nothing in its narrative says so. The model did nothing wrong. The table was not ready to answer the question. Reminding only works if the record is right. "Data first" means something specific here: a department field that is required, filled from a controlled list, with a named owner. That is what a data model is, and it is the next beat.

The habit that comes out of that demo, usable on Monday with any tool, is one follow-up question typed every time: "How many rows did you use, how many did you leave out, and why?" Then: "Which rows were you unsure about?" This is not the model being sly. Every reporting system drops what it was not told to count, and always has; the difference is that this one will tell you if asked. A report that cannot answer those two questions is not finished.

**Hand-off:** the model can be reminded, and the reminder can be wrong. The next ten minutes are about what the record has to be, and what it costs to hand over.

## Block 4. The data underneath (10 minutes)

Which AI model matters most? Wrong question. The data model.

**The record has to be right, and shared.** Every office tracks the same things, a sponsor, a PI, an award, a budget, and every system names them differently. Every report starts by reconciling them, and nothing built at one institution runs at the next. A shared data model is the fix. The AI4RA Unified Data Model is a draft of one, written by a small team at one institution: forty tables with plain names, an Award with an Award_Title, a Start_Date, and a Funds_Obligated_Amount; a Proposal that points at three organizations by role, the sponsor, the submitting unit, and the administering unit; a Personnel record a research administrator could read without training. The ask is for readers, not adopters: does it describe an award the way you would, what does your office track that is missing, what do you call differently.

**The record has to live somewhere governed.** Keep the raw sources so you can prove what they said, translate each one to the shared model once, and let the model answer from the reconciled copy, not from whichever spreadsheet was open. The difference is a tool that is confidently wrong versus a pipeline that is honestly incomplete.

**Reminding has a cost.** Everything you hand the model, it reads. The room has been doing this all morning: the shared workshop key points at the University of Idaho's own model gateway, a campus-run service in front of models the university has chosen, which is the hybrid option below made concrete, and the reason the paste rule could be as relaxed as a bulletin board. For Thamus the cost of the record was forgetting. For a machine, the cost is exposure. So, where does the model run? Vendor cloud, with the best models and the least visibility; local, where the data never leaves the building; or hybrid. If cloud, two tiers, and the talk is explicit about both. For practice on public text, the kind of thing the bulletin-board rule allows, a personal key on a free tier is fine; that is what the Monday Promptulus exercise uses, and nothing from your institution's systems goes through it. For anything exported from those systems, an awards table, a workbook, a document from a file, use the account your institution has vetted, never a personal consumer login. Vendors' business and API tiers do carry no-training terms, but the institutional agreement, the contract the university signs with the vendor, is the one someone at your institution has read, and it adds what a tier alone does not: a named contact and an audit trail, meaning a log of who sent what and when that the institution can review if a question is ever asked. Most universities already hold such an agreement for Microsoft or Google even if nobody in the research office knows it; the chief information officer's office can say in one email whether the university has a Microsoft enterprise or campus agreement or a Google Workspace for Education agreement, and whether Copilot or Gemini is covered under it. Some data may not leave under any agreement: student records under FERPA, health information under HIPAA, export-controlled technical data, personally identifiable information, anything a sponsor's terms restrict. Your institution's data classification policy is the authority, and if it has none, the bulletin-board rule from the opening is the interim policy.

**Hand-off:** the record can be right, shared, governed, and safely handed over, and the answer can still be wrong. The block ends on the question the next one answers: who owns the answer?

## Block 5. Accountability (9 minutes)

Four questions before any task goes to a model, in thirty seconds:

1. Can you hand it everything it needs? If the answer depends on your award terms, your policy, or your data, those have to be in the question. If they cannot be, the task is "data first," not "AI now."
2. Can you check the answer cheaply? Against a source, a known sample, a second run, or your own eyes, in under a minute. If checking costs as much as doing, you have saved nothing and added a risk.
3. Who is accountable for the result? A name, a role, a signature. If the honest answer is "the AI," stop.
4. Does it have to be the same tomorrow? Audit samples, determinations, and anything a reviewer might repeat need a reproducible method. The model gives a different answer each run, so reproducibility has to come from the method.

Then the sort. Nine real tasks, three bins, AI now, data first, keep human: pull deadlines and page limits out of a solicitation; draft a budget justification from a completed budget; compare a subaward budget to its justification and flag mismatches; report federal funding by department for the Vice President for Research; list every active award with an overdue effort certification; predict which proposals are most likely to be funded; decide whether a questionable cost transfer is allowable; negotiate a disputed IP clause with an industry sponsor; tell a PI their proposal missed the deadline. Two test questions place any task: if someone asked how this was done, would the answer matter? Could a colleague re-run it and get the same result? Where both answers are "not really," AI can lead. Where either is "yes," a person stays in the loop. The room sorts by show of hands, then the answers are revealed as a position to argue with, not a fact. AI now: the solicitation, the budget justification, the subaward comparison, because the document holding the answer can be handed over and the result checked against it in a minute. Data first: funding by department, overdue effort certifications, funding prediction, because the answer lives in the award system, the HR system, and the chart of accounts, and the model can see none of them; those tasks are blocked on data, not on AI. Keep human: allowability, the IP negotiation, and the conversation with a PI who is about to be upset, because each exists so that an accountable person can say they decided it; AI can prepare material for all three and own none of them. Three of the nine land in "data first," which is the sort's own argument for the data model the room just saw.

**For the attendee whose institution has no AI guidance,** the block ends with how to start one, because that is the real answer. The four questions, the two test questions, the bulletin-board rule, and the vetted-account rule are a defensible one-page interim policy for your own desk from Monday. The institutional version starts with three people in a room: whoever owns research compliance (usually under the Vice President for Research), whoever owns information security (usually under the chief information officer), and someone from the research office who does the work. If you do not know the names, your institution's policy library, searched for "data classification" and "acceptable use," names the offices that own those documents, and those offices are the people. Start from documents you already have: the data classification policy if there is one, and the enterprise agreement the university almost certainly already holds with Microsoft or Google, which decides which tools are in bounds before anyone writes a word about AI. Borrow definitions rather than drafting them; the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act, NIH's data science strategic plan, and recent university guidelines (the University of Utah's, from September 2026, is a clean example) each define AI in a sentence. The first document is one page: which tools, which data, what review, who signs. Put a six-month review date on it, because the tools will have changed.

Filled in, for one office, it reads about like this. *Tools: the Copilot and Gemini accounts under the university's Microsoft and Google agreements; no personal accounts for work. Data: anything that could be posted on a bulletin board; nothing with salaries, student or patient information, export-controlled detail, sponsor-confidential terms, or unfunded proposal content. Review: a named person reads every output before it leaves the office, and any task that must be reproducible is done with a method, not a chat. Signature: the research office director. Review date: six months from today.* Eight sentences, and it is more than most institutions have. If your institution forbids personal keys and holds no enterprise agreement, that one page is also the request for one, and the sort, the policy reading, and the UDM review need no key at all.

**Hand-off:** you know what the model is, how to ask it, what to hand it, and who owns the answer. Now the smallest thing you can do with that on Monday.

## Block 6. Start small (7 minutes)

Three recipes. The first two touch files exported from your systems, so they run on the institution's account, never a personal one; the third is practice on public text and a personal key is fine. All three share the second rule: read what it wrote before you trust it. The bulletin-board rule from the opening is the data rule throughout.

- **An afternoon's dashboard.** Export a CSV of proposals by sponsor or awards by department that passes the bulletin-board rule: award titles, sponsors, departments, amounts, and PI names on funded awards are public record; salaries, personnel data, and unfunded proposal content are not in the file. Paste it into the chat model your institution's account covers (the demo uses Claude; any model that writes a web page will do) and ask for an interactive dashboard: totals by sponsor type, awards ending within 90 days, a department filter. It writes the page in about a minute. Then check two numbers against a pivot table, or ask the spreadsheet add-in from the next recipe to total them. It is a prototype for a conversation with leadership, not a system of record, and the blank-department lesson applies. One more rule: the page the model writes has your CSV embedded in it, so the file is the spreadsheet in another costume and travels under the same rules.
- **AI inside the spreadsheet you already use.** The add-in your Microsoft 365 or Google agreement already covers sits beside the open workbook. Ask it to explain an F&A formula in plain language, write a formula that flags awards ending within 90 days, classify sponsors as federal, industry, or nonprofit and list the rows it was unsure about. Read every formula. Once you have read it, it is yours.
- **Your Promptulus task, for real.** Run the task you worked in Block 2 on Monday, with a colleague checking the result.

Each bigger step depends on the one below it: a clean spreadsheet before a dashboard, a dashboard before a shared data model, a shared model before any tool that runs across offices. Pick one of the three now, and write it down; that is the thing you take home.

**Hand-off:** small starts are checked by hand each time. When the same task repeats a hundred times, you want a workflow you can check once.

## Block 7. Vandalizer (11 minutes)

Two demos of Vandalizer, a document extraction and review platform built for research administrators by the presenters' own project, so the disclosure from the opening applies here most of all: judge it by the checks, not by who is showing it. Extract the PI, the dates, the budget, and the terms from an award notice into a structured record, with the source passage shown for every value. Then check a proposal against its solicitation and catch the mismatch. The point is the reusable workflow: same prompt, same steps, so accuracy can be measured once and trusted many times. The block ends with three ways to check output you did not write: inspect it directly against the source, keep the trail of how it was produced, and test the tool on cases where you already know the answer before trusting it on ones where you do not. As tools take more steps on their own, inspection carries less of the load and the other two carry more. That third check is also how to evaluate any tool, this one included: run twenty documents you have already processed by hand, count the misses, and decide.

## Close (4 minutes)

Three bins for every task on your desk: automate, augment, or leave alone. Then back to the king, for the first time since the story in Block 3. Thamus warned that writing would ruin memory. He is remembered only because Plato wrote him down. The machine needs to remember less and look things up more; that was the first ninety minutes. People need to keep enough in their own heads to judge what comes back, which is the half of Thamus's warning that still stands, and that is Bert's hour: reasoning puzzles where a story carries the rule a grid leaves open, arguments by analogy, and a structured ethical discussion format to run at your own institution.

## What a good Monday looks like

- You have found your institution's AI guidance and data classification policy, or confirmed there is none, adopted the one-page interim policy from the accountability block for your own desk, and asked the three people named there for a meeting.
- You have run your Promptulus task for real, with a colleague checking the result.
- You have sorted five tasks from your own desk into the three bins and talked about one with your supervisor.
- You have read the UDM for ten minutes and sent one sentence about it to the AI4RA community list.
